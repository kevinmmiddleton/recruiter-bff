# Answers and Scripts

Starting points for the hard parts of an interview. Every script gets rewritten in the user's own voice, with their real facts. If a script needs a fact the user hasn't given, leave a placeholder and ask. Never fill in pay, employment status, or work authorization for them.

## How to answer, in general

- **Lead with the answer,** then the proof. The first sentence should work on its own.
- **Under 150 words** for most answers; a told story runs about 90 seconds.
- **Two-part questions ("and"):** answer the second half first. It's the half people forget, and the first half is still fresh for the interviewer.
- **No warm-up phrases.** Drop "great question," "that's a really interesting question," and "happy to walk you through some examples." Start with the substance.
- **Match the room.** A recruiter wants three clear sentences. A hiring manager wants the decision and why. An engineer wants the evidence and its limits. An executive wants the result in their metric and the cheapest way to test the idea.
- **Same story everywhere.** Claims, numbers, and dates must match across rounds and the written application. Reread the guide's "say carefully" notes before each call.
- **Honest gaps:** "Not yet. The closest I've done is ..." Then the proof. No apology, no spin.
- **Never run the user's own work down,** even in a "what would you do differently" answer. Name the lesson, not a failing.

## Awkward questions

Include only the ones that apply to this user.

**Why are you leaving?** (or why did you leave)
Forward-looking and short. What they want next, tied to this role; never a complaint about the last employer.
> "I'd done what I came to do at [company]: [one-line result]. What I want next is [the thing this role offers], and that's why this one caught my eye."
If they were laid off: say it plainly, without drama. "My role was part of a layoff in [month]. Since then I've been [truthful current activity]."

**What have you been doing since your last role?** (a gap)
The truthful "current" line: what they've actually been doing, in plain words. Never "consulting" without clients.
> "Since [month] I've been [real activity: building X, taking a course in Y, caring for family, searching full time], and I've used the time to [one concrete thing]."

**Short stints on the resume**
One line per stint, no defensiveness, then the pattern that makes sense of it.
> "[Company] was acquired six months after I joined and the team was folded in. [Company] was a contract I took on purpose. The common thread is [what they were building toward]."

**"You might be overqualified"**
Show they've thought about it and want this specific job, without lowering their own work.
> "I get why it reads that way. What draws me is [specific part of this role], and the scope here is the kind of work I want to be doing day to day, not a step on the way to something else."

**"Are you currently employed?" / current situation**
The user decides what to say; offer the honest version in their words. Never answer this for them.

**"Have you applied here before?"**
Yes is fine. Say what's changed since.
> "I did, for [role] in [year]. Since then I've [what's new], and this role is a closer match to what I do best."

**"What are your hesitations about this role?"**
Name one real, reasonable question as a question, not a fear.
> "The one thing I'd want to understand better is [a real open question from the watch-fors]. Can you tell me how that works today?"

## Pay

The user's own pay line lives in the profile ("What I say when asked about pay"). Use it. If it's not set, ask what they want to say before the round; don't guess.

**The range is posted.**
> "The posted range of [range] works for me, depending on the full package."
If it's low against their floor: "I saw the posted range. For the scope you've described, I'd be looking toward the top of it."

**The range isn't posted, and they ask first.**
Turn it around once, politely, then answer if pressed.
> "I'd love to understand the range you've budgeted for this role first, so we're working from the same place."
If pressed: "Based on the scope and what I'm seeing for similar roles, I'm targeting [the user's range]."

**Their range is below the user's floor.**
Say it early and plainly, so nobody wastes a month.
> "Thanks for sharing that. It's below what I'm targeting, which is [floor or range]. Is there flexibility in the level or the range?"

**Panelists vs. the recruiter.**
Pay is a recruiter conversation. If a panelist or hiring manager asks, keep it short and redirect.
> "I've shared my range with [recruiter]. I'm much more focused right now on whether I'm the right fit for the work."

## Timeline

Tell recruiters your timeline early. It moves processes and prevents surprises.

> "I want to be upfront: I'm in later stages with a couple of other companies and expect to have decisions in the next two to three weeks. I'm very interested in this role, so I wanted you to know."

Only say what's true. Count the calendar: if the user has an offer deadline, work out the dates for every other process and say what they'd need to hear, by when.

## Live case method

For product, strategy, or "how would you approach this" rounds:

1. **Clarify first.** Restate the goal in one line. Ask who the user is, what success means, and what's off the table. Write the answer down.
2. **Find where people drop out.** Walk the user journey and name the step where the most people stop or the most value is lost. That's where to spend the time.
3. **Ideas, easiest to undo first.** Generate options, then order them by how cheap and reversible they are. A copy change before a rebuild.
4. **The test and the safety metric.** For the top idea: how you'd test it, the metric you expect to move, and the one you'd watch to make sure nothing breaks.
5. **Close with a call.** "I'd start with [X] because [Y]. We'd know it's working by [Z]." Then say what would change your mind.

Think out loud the whole way. The interviewer is grading the reasoning more than the answer. If they push back, treat it as new information, not an attack.

**Take-homes:** reuse their section names, check every requirement in the brief before submitting, list your key assumptions so "what if we changed this?" has an answer, and ask about level and pay before investing many hours. A 30/60/90 plan is fair to ask for; real work they'd normally pay for should be paid.

## Questions that reveal the real job

- What's the biggest problem on this team you haven't gotten to yet?
- How will you measure whether this hire worked, a year from now?
- How would someone fail in this role?
- What did the last person in this seat do well, and what would you change?
- How do decisions get made when product, design, and engineering disagree?
- How much time do leaders here spend with customers?
