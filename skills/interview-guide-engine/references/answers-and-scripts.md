# Answers and Scripts

Starting points for the hard parts of an interview. Every script gets rewritten in the user's own voice, with their real facts. If a script needs a fact the user hasn't given, leave a placeholder and ask. These are spoken lines for calls: never fill in pay, employment status, or work authorization on a written application for the user.

## How to answer, in general

- **Lead with the answer,** then the proof. The first sentence should work on its own.
- **Under 150 words** for most answers; a told story runs about 90 seconds.
- **Two-part questions ("and"):** answer the second half first. The half people drop ("what was hard about it," "where did it break," "what would worry me about hiring you") is usually what's being tested, and it holds the best material. Leading with it makes the rest sound like context, not spin.
- **No filler.** Drop "great question," "I'm always up for a challenge," and closing on "happy to walk through some examples." Start with the substance; stop when it's said.
- **Match the room.** A recruiter wants three clear sentences. A hiring manager wants the decision and why. An engineer wants the evidence and its limits. An executive wants the result in their metric, what you wouldn't do, and the cheapest way to test the idea.
- **Same story everywhere.** Assume notes travel between interviewers. Claims, numbers, and dates must match across rounds and the written application. Reread the story bank's "Say carefully" lines before each call.
- **Honest gaps:** "Not yet. The closest I've done is ..." Then the proof. No apology, no spin.
- **Never run the user's own work down,** even in a "what would you do differently" answer. Name the lesson, not a failing.

## Awkward questions

One or two sentences, then stop. No apology, never criticize a past employer, and the same line with every interviewer. Include only the ones that apply to this user.

**Why are you leaving?** One sentence on what happened, one on what's next.
> "I've done what I came to do at [company]: [one-line result]. Next I want [the thing this role offers], and that's why this one caught my eye."
Laid off: "My role was part of a layoff in [month]. Since then I've been [truthful current activity]." Plainly, without drama.

**What have you been doing since your last role?** The truthful "current" line: what actually happened and can be checked. Never "consulting" without paying clients.
> "Since [month] I've been [building X / studying Y / caring for family / searching full time], and I've [one concrete, checkable thing]."

**Short stints.** One honest circumstance per stint (an acquisition, a restructure, a layoff), never fit or performance. Then point to the longest run, then say what they want, unprompted.
> "[Company] was acquired six months after I joined, and the team was folded in. My longer runs, [N] years at [company], are how I work when things are stable. I'm looking for a place to stay a while."
Keep the private reasons separate from this sayable version.

**"You might be overqualified."** Show it's deliberate, and don't negotiate level on the call.
> "I'm deliberate about that. I want to own a meaty problem and be close to the people who use what I build. The team and the work matter more to me than the title."

**"Are you currently employed?"** Never answered in writing for the user. On a call, the user decides; if they're employed and want to say so: "I am. I'm being selective, and this role fits what I'm looking for." Don't volunteer, don't dodge, don't name other companies.

**"Have you applied here before?"** Don't raise it. If they do:
> "I have. What I've done since, [the new work], is a much closer match to this role."
Several applications at one company: "I applied broadly here because [the work] is what I do best. This role is the closest match because [reason]."

**"What are your hesitations about this role?"** Two real ones, briefly: one about the company (something obviously true, like a crowded market) and one about the user (a domain to learn). Then stop. Claiming none reads worse than naming two.

**Conflicts** (a past employer now owned by their competitor, a location question): raise it early, unprompted. "What I bring is industry fluency, nothing proprietary." Never use a past employer's internal numbers.

## Pay

Spoken lines only. The user's own line lives in the profile ("What I say when asked about pay"), with their floor, comfortable, and ideal numbers. Know all three before any call; if they aren't set, ask before the round, in a reply that doesn't also end with an offer.

**Which line to use:** when the posting lists a range, compare it with the user's own line. If their target sits inside the posted range, "The posted range works for me" is enough, and their own line is the answer if pushed for a number. If their target is near or above the top, use their own line, or the near-the-top version below. Never give two different numbers to the same company.

**The range is posted.**
> "The posted range works for me."
If asked for a number: one inside the band, near the top. Being coy about a public band gains nothing. If the top of the band is about the user's current pay, tell them privately that the upside here is the role and equity, not salary.

**The range isn't posted.**
> "What's the range for the role? Happy to tell you if we're in the same neighborhood."
If pushed: "I'm targeting [low] to [high] base, depending on equity and the full package." Then ask for their band. A recruiter who won't share one is telling you something. Don't anchor low. If theirs comes in under: "That's lighter than where I can land. Is there flexibility at the offer stage?"

**A very wide band.**
> "Where does this role actually sit in that range, and what separates the top from the bottom?"
Pin down the level and the title.

**Below the floor.** Decide before dialing which call this is:
- A friendly networking call: learn what you can, and skip any long take-home.
- Or say it early and kindly: "I've liked what I've seen of [company]. I'll be honest, my target is above what I'd expect for this band. Is there any flex, or should we stay in touch for something more senior?"
Ask about pay before agreeing to any long take-home.

**A panelist or hiring manager asks.** Pay is the recruiter's conversation. "The range works for me," or "I've shared my range with [recruiter]; I'm more focused right now on whether I'm the right fit for the work." Never negotiate in the room.

## Timeline

Say it in the first five minutes, as information, not a threat. It speeds processes up.

> "I want to be upfront: I'm in a few active processes and have a timeline forming, so I'd rather know early whether yours can move."
With a real offer: "I have an offer with a decision date of [date], so I wanted you to know where things stand."

Then ask theirs: "What does the timeline look like, and how many rounds are left after this?" Count the calendar: if four rounds over six weeks can't fit inside the user's window, thank them warmly and say so. Don't name other companies. With an outside recruiter, ask for the hiring manager's own read on timing.

Only say what's true. If this is the user's only active process, don't invent urgency. "I'm actively interviewing and can move quickly" is honest and enough; or say nothing about other processes and just ask for their timeline.

## Live case method

Out loud, as you go:

1. **Clarify first, for a minute or two.** Who's the user, what's the goal or metric, what's the constraint, what's been tried? Restate the prompt. Ask; don't lecture.
2. **Frame it as a leak.** Where do people drop out, and what are customers already saying (reviews, search terms, support tickets)?
3. **Two or three ideas, easiest to undo first.** Pick one segment and say why. A copy change before a rebuild.
4. **Pick one: the test, the success metric, and the guardrail metric** (the one that tells you nothing broke).
5. **Recommend:** "I'd do [X] first, because [Y], and we'd know by [Z]." Then invite them to poke holes.

What lands, especially with executives: one rough sentence on the money first, then the recommendation, what you *wouldn't* do, and the cheapest test. Name your own bias up front. Turn a false either-or into a menu (A, B, C, each with some of the hard work; the real question is how much). Get estimates before committing. Check scope ("Does that match what you had in mind?"), and end with a 30-second recap. If they cut your capacity, cut the build and keep the measurement.

**Take-homes:** the brief is the grading sheet. Check every requirement, reuse their section names, list your key assumptions so "what if we changed this?" has an answer, and grade it against the brief before submitting. Ask about level and pay before investing many hours. A 30/60/90 plan is fair to ask for; real work they'd normally pay for should be paid. If they have rules about AI use, follow them.

## Questions that reveal the real job

- What's the biggest problem in [area] you haven't gotten to yet?
- What would make you glad you hired someone into this role, a year from now?
- How would someone fail in this role?
- What does a normal week look like for this person?
- How do decisions get made when product, design, and engineering disagree?
- What's one thing that keeps you here, and one thing you'd change?

## Screening them back

- "Is this a new seat or a backfill?" If a backfill: "What happened with the last person?"
- How long have the hiring manager and their boss been there? If both are under about 18 months, the user's champion could leave soon.
- "Tell me about a real disagreement on the team." Does the story have two sides? Does the other person still work there?
- "What happened after the last project that didn't work?" Listen for blame or learning.
- Ask to meet a future peer before the final round. A refusal is information. Ask the peer what happens when someone disagrees with the manager.
- How much turnover has the team had in the last year? At a startup: stage, funding, runway, team size.

Red flags: answering the user's real experience with hypotheticals; "we're like a family." The answers decide where the user invests past the recruiter screen.
